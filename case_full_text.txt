01. Problem Statement 
Multi-Hazard Smart Campus Safety & Response Grid (SCS-RG) 
Design and build a full-stack IoT system that senses multiple hazard types across UFT-
B's technical labs, fuses them into a risk-ranked live incident feed and gives campus 
security a command dashboard they can trust during a real, simultaneous, multi-zone 
emergency. 
Target Users: Campus Security Staff, Facilities Admins 
Round 1 Total Marks: 200 (160 core, including Documentation & Presentation, + up to 40 
Bonus) 
The system must: 
▸ Monitor at least 3 independent zones for fire, gas, water-level/flood and pres-
ence/occupancy signals. 
▸ Compute a per-zone risk score from those signals. 
▸ Rank zones by response priority when multiple are critical at once, so staff know 
which one to check first. 
▸ Run through a real backend, a real database and a real frontend dashboard that 
security staff would actually trust during a live incident.  
Scenario 
UFTB's campus runs on its technical labs — the IoT Lab, Robotics Lab, Software Lab, 
Data Science Lab and the Server Room — but has no unified way to detect and respond 
to hazards across them. A soldering-related fire risk in the IoT Lab's hardware bench, 
an overheating GPU rack in the Data Science Lab and an AC condensate leak near the 
Server Room's cooling unit are all currently handled the same way: someone notices, 
someone shouts, someone calls someone else. There's no sensing, no automatic re-
sponse and no way for campus security to know, in real time, which lab needs help 
first when several things go wrong at once.

02. Glossary of Key Terms 
Use these terms the same way throughout your documentation and video — consistent, 
precise use of this vocabulary makes your submission much easier for judges to follow.  
Term 
Meaning 
Zone 
An independently-monitored area (a lab or room) with its own 
sensors and its own SAFE / WARNING / CRITICAL state. 
SAFE / WARNING / 
CRITICAL 
The three-level state of a zone, derived from its risk score. SAFE: no 
meaningful hazard signal. WARNING: elevated signal, visual no-
tice only. CRITICAL: hazard confirmed — triggers local response 
and enters the priority queue. 
Risk Score 
A single number (e.g. 0-100) computed per zone from its raw sensor 
readings via a weighted formula — not the raw sensor values di-
rectly. 
Debounce 
Requiring a signal to persist for a minimum time or minimum con-
secutive readings before treating it as real, so a single noise spike 
doesn't cause a false alarm. 
Risk Fusion 
Combining a zone's fire, gas, water-level and occupancy signals 
into that zone's single risk score. 
Priority Ranking 
The ordering of currently-critical zones by urgency, so staff know 
which one to respond to first. 
Server-Side Computa-
tion 
Calculating the risk score on the backend from raw sensor inputs, 
rather than trusting a value reported directly by the zone node. 
Race Condition 
A bug where the outcome depends on unpredictable timing be-
tween two things happening at nearly the same moment. 
Referential Integrity 
A database property ensuring relationships between tables stay 
valid — an Incident should never point to a Zone that no longer ex-
ists. 
RBAC (Role-Based Ac-
cess Control) 
Restricting what a user can do based on their role, enforced by the 
backend/API — not just by hiding a button in the interface. 
Idle State 
The system's resting state where all zones are SAFE and no inci-
dents are open. 
Edge Case 
A scenario outside the simple expected flow — sensor failure, two 
things happening at once, network or power loss — that the system 
needs to handle correctly. 
Predicted Risk 
A machine-learning-derived estimate (bonus feature) of how likely 
a zone is to become CRITICAL soon, kept separate from the live risk 
score and never allowed to trigger physical actuation on its own.

1. A zone node samples its sensors on a fixed interval — for example, every 500ms to 
1 second is a reasonable starting point. 
2. The zone node sends its raw readings to the backend's ingestion endpoint. 
3. The backend checks the payload is well-formed, then computes the zone's risk 
score itself using the formula in Section 13 — the zone node never reports its own 
risk score or state, only raw numbers. 
4. If the computed score crosses a threshold, the zone's state changes. This is saved 
to the database and pushed out to every connected dashboard. 
5. If the new state is CRITICAL, the backend also sends a command back down to 
that zone node to trigger the buzzer, LED and relay. 
6. If more than one zone is CRITICAL at the same time, the priority ranking engine 
recalculates the ordered list and the dashboard updates to show it. 
7. When staff acknowledges an incident from the dashboard, the backend records 
who acknowledged it and when — only one acknowledgment should actually be 
recorded if two people try at once. 
8. When a zone's risk score drops back below the threshold, the system logs the re-
covery and that zone returns to idle.

05. Evaluation Approach 
Every test case below is checked against your submitted system — via your video 
demonstration and, where relevant, by reviewing your source code. Marks are earned 
by your system actually doing what's described, shown clearly in the video with nar-
ration explaining what's happening on screen — not by a written description of what 
it's supposed to do. 
Where a test case has several lettered required behaviors, try to show each one as its 
own short, clearly labeled clip or segment in your video rather than one long unstruc-
tured recording — it makes judging faster and fairer and it's also just easier for you to 
be sure you've covered everything.

06. Section A: Hardware & Sensing [40 marks]   
Test Case 1: Fire/Flame Sensor Detection [8 marks]   
Conditions:  One flame sensor per zone — for example, guarding the IoT Lab or Robot-
ics Lab's soldering station or the Server Room's electrical panel. You decide the detec-
tion range and the debounce window. 
Required Tests: 
▸ (a) No flame present → Expected: SAFE 
▸ (b) A brief flicker shorter than your debounce window → Expected: no trigger 
▸ (c) A sustained flame longer than your debounce window → Expected: CRITICAL 
contribution to the risk score 
▸ (d) The flame source is removed → Expected: the score decays back to SAFE within 
a recovery window you define 
How you might approach this:  Sample the sensor every 100-200ms; treat a flame as 
"real" only after N consecutive HIGH readings (e.g. 5 readings = 1 second); on removal, 
let the score decay linearly over a few seconds rather than snapping instantly back to 
zero. 
Evaluation:  detection accuracy (3), debounce correctness (3), recovery behavior (2). 
Test Case 2: Gas Sensor Detection [8 marks]   
Conditions:  Most relevant in the IoT Lab and Robotics Lab, where soldering fumes and 
battery off-gassing are realistic. 
Required Tests: 
▸ (a) Baseline clean air → Expected: near-zero contribution 
▸ (b) Gradually rising concentration → Expected: the contribution rises proportion-
ally, not in a single jump 
▸ (c) Concentration crosses your CRITICAL threshold → Expected: the zone escalates 
▸ (d) The first ~30 seconds after power-on (sensor warm-up) → Expected: no false 
trigger during this period 
How you might approach this:  Normalize the raw analog reading to a 0.0-1.0 scale 
based on your sensor's datasheet range, rather than using the raw ADC value directly; 
ignore readings during the first 30 seconds after boot. 
Evaluation:  graded response accuracy (3), threshold accuracy (3), warm-up handling 
(2).

Test Case 3: Water-Level / Flood Sensor Detection [8 marks]   
Conditions:  Most relevant in the Server Room and Data Science Lab, where an AC con-
densate or coolant leak is the realistic flood risk. 
Required Tests:  
▸ (a) Dry → Expected: SAFE 
▸ (b) Rising water level over time → Expected: proportional risk contribution 
▸ (c) Level crosses your CRITICAL threshold → Expected: the zone escalates 
▸ (d) The sensor is wetted, then cleared → Expected: correct reset 
How you might approach this:  Place the sensor at the lowest point under the AC unit 
or cooling equipment, since that's where a real leak would pool first; test the reset by 
physically drying the sensor. 
Evaluation:  accuracy (3), graded response (3), reset correctness (2). 
Test Case 4: PIR Presence / Occupancy Detection [8 marks]   
Required Tests: 
▸ (a) Empty zone → Expected: occupancy = false 
▸ (b) A person enters → Expected: occupancy = true within about 1 second 
▸ (c) A brief exit and re-entry within ~2 seconds → Expected: this does not spam the 
log with repeated events 
▸ (d) Sensor disconnected → Expected: the zone shows OFFLINE, not silently reported 
as empty 
How you might approach this:  Most PIR modules already have a built-in retrigger delay 
you can tune; only log an occupancy change event if the new state holds for at least 1-
2 seconds. 
Evaluation:  detection reliability (3), flicker handling (3), offline detection (2).

no cutoff. 
Required Tests: 
▸ (a) A zone crosses into CRITICAL → Expected: full response within 1 second, logged 
with a timestamp

07. Section B: Backend System [35 marks]   
Test Case 6: Real-Time Ingestion & Risk Fusion [11 marks]   
Conditions:  The risk score must be computed on the backend from raw sensor values 
— a zone node should never be able to simply report "I am SAFE" and have that trusted 
directly. 
Required Tests: 
▸ (a) A zone sends raw sensor values → Expected: the backend computes the risk 
score using the formula documented in Section 13 
▸ (b) A malformed or out-of-range payload → Expected: rejected with a clear error, not 
silently absorbed 
▸ (c) Two hazards cross threshold in the same zone at once → Expected: the com-
bined score reflects both, computed from the real formula 
▸ (d) The same reading arrives twice (network retry) → Expected: it is not counted 
twice 
▸ (e) All zones send readings at their normal interval at once → Expected: none are 
dropped or noticeably delayed 
How you might approach this:  A simple sequence number or short time-window de-
duplication check catches (d); a lightweight backend framework (Flask, FastAPI, Ex-
press) with async handling comfortably handles this for (e). 
Evaluation:  server-side computation correctness (4), input validation (3), multi-hazard 
fusion (2), duplicate handling (1), concurrent ingestion (1).

Required Tests: 
▸ (a) Three zones change state within 1 second of each other → Expected: none of the 
updates are dropped 
▸ (b) Two staff members acknowledge the same alert within milliseconds → Ex-

▸ (c) A zone rapidly flips CRITICAL-SAFE-CRITICAL over a few seconds → Expected: 
no flood of duplicate incidents 
▸ (d) An alert already acknowledged and resolved triggers again later → Expected: 
this creates a new incident record

09. Section D: Database Design [20 marks]   
Test Case 17: Schema Design [7 marks]   
Conditions:  A schema with clearly related tables for Zones, Sensors, Readings, Inci-
dents, Acknowledgments and Users_Roles — not one large table holding everything. 
State primary and foreign keys explicitly, e.g. Readings.zone_id -> Zones.id. 
How you might approach this:  Zones describes what exists, Readings is the raw sensor 
history, Incidents is the derived "something happened" record, and Acknowledg-
ments/Users_Roles handle who did what — keeping these separate lets you answer 
both "what did the sensor read" and "what did the system decide". 
Evaluation:  normalization and relationship correctness (4), explicit key design (3). 
Test Case 18: Concurrency & Integrity [5 marks]   
Required Tests: 
▸ (a) Ten simultaneous sensor writes across different zones → Expected: no data loss 
▸ (b) Attempting to delete a zone that still has open incidents → Expected: blocked or 
archived 
▸ (c) A reading arrives with a timestamp earlier than the zone's last recorded reading 
→ Expected: doesn't scramble the timeline 
How you might approach this:  For (b) a foreign key constraint with ON DELETE RE-
STRICT (or an explicit archive flag instead of a real delete) is a one-line fix. 
Evaluation:  concurrent write safety (2), integrity constraint demonstrated (2), 
timestamp handling (1). 
Test Case 19: Query Performance [4 marks]   
Conditions:  The query "all CRITICAL incidents in the last 24 hours across all zones" 
returns quickly even against a larger seeded dataset (e.g. 10,000+ reading rows). State 
which column(s) you indexed and why. 
How you might approach this:  An index on the Incidents table's (status, created_at) 
columns together is usually the single biggest win for this query. 
Evaluation:  query and indexing design (3), demonstrated reasoning (1).

13. Worked Example: Risk Fusion Formula 
This shows the kind of specificity your own formula should have — adapt or replace the actual 
numbers with your own reasoning. 
Here, fire is weighted highest because it's the fastest-escalating and most damaging hazard in 
a lab full of electronics. Occupancy is weighted lowest on its own, since an empty zone with a 
real hazard is still dangerous to responders and equipment — but occupancy matters more 
when ranking between zones (Section 08, Test Case 12) than it does for a single zone's own 
score. Use whatever weights make sense for your own reasoning, as long as you explain them. 
 
risk_score(zone) =
 
    
w_fire   * fire_signal (0 or 1, after debounce)
 
  + w_gas    * gas_level_norm
 (0.0 to 1.0, normalized to sensor range)
 
  + w_water  * water_level_norm (0.0 to 1.0)
 
  + w_occ    * occupancy_factor (0.0 to 1.0, higher if the zone is occupied)
 
  
  where: 
w_fire = 40, w_gas = 25, w_water = 20, w_occ = 15
 
  (these weights add up to 100, so risk_score naturally falls in 0
- 100) 
  
  Classification:
 
    
risk_score < 30           
- > SAFE 
    
30 <= risk_score < 65     
- > WARNING 
    
risk_score >= 65          
- > CRITICAL
